How to Use Your Golf Handicap When Playing Against Others

Getting strokes, giving strokes, and making any round competitive — here's how it actually works.

Having a handicap is one thing. Knowing how to actually use it when you tee up against someone with a different index is another. This is where a lot of recreational golfers get stuck — you know your number, they know their number, and then someone asks "so what are we playing?" and it gets awkward fast.

Here's the practical guide to making handicaps work in casual rounds and match play.

The basic concept: net scores

When two golfers with different handicaps play against each other, the goal is to level the field so both players have a genuine chance of winning. This is done through net scores — your gross score (actual strokes) minus the handicap strokes you receive on specific holes.

The lower handicap player gives strokes to the higher handicap player. If you're a 15 and your opponent is a 5, you receive 10 strokes from them — one stroke on each of the 10 hardest holes on the course. Those holes are identified by the "handicap" row on the scorecard, ranked 1-18 from hardest to easiest.

Converting your index to a course handicap

Your handicap index (the number attached to your GHIN account) isn't directly what you use on the course. You first convert it to a course handicap, which accounts for the specific difficulty of the course and tees you're playing that day.

The formula: Course Handicap = Handicap Index x (Slope Rating / 113) + (Course Rating - Par)

The course handicap calculator does this instantly. On a standard course, your course handicap will be close to your index. On a very difficult course it'll be higher; on an easier one, lower. On a hard course with a slope of 135, a 15 index might become an 18 playing handicap — three extra strokes matter in a close match.

The most common mistake in casual play: using your index number directly as your playing handicap without converting it for the course. On a difficult track, you're shortchanging yourself out of strokes you're entitled to.

Which holes you get strokes on

Look at the "HCP" or "Handicap" row on any scorecard. Each hole is ranked 1 to 18, where 1 is the hardest hole and 18 is the easiest. If you're receiving 10 strokes, you get one extra stroke on holes ranked 1 through 10.

On those holes, your net score is one better than your gross. Make a bogey on a handicap-1 hole when you're receiving a stroke? Your net score is par — the same score your low-handicap opponent needs to tie that hole. That's the system working exactly as intended.

Common formats and how handicaps apply

Stroke play: Count up net strokes across all 18 holes. Lowest net score wins. Simple, works for any group size.

Match play (hole by hole): Each hole is won, lost, or halved based on net scores. Knowing which holes you have a stroke on matters — it changes when you should play aggressively and when to protect a net par.

Stableford: Points based on net score relative to par. Net double bogey or worse scores 0, net bogey scores 1, net par scores 2, net birdie scores 3. Common in club competitions.

Skins: Each hole has a value. Win a hole outright on net score and take the skin. Ties carry the skin forward. Great format for groups where the handicap range is wide.

Practical setup tips before you tee off

  • Agree on whether you're playing full handicap or a percentage (many groups use 80-90% to tighten competition between players who know each other's tendencies).
  • Check the scorecard handicap row before the round so you know your stroke holes going in — don't figure it out on the green.
  • If someone in the group doesn't have an official index, agree on a reasonable estimate beforehand. The handicap estimator gives a quick ballpark from typical scores.
  • For groups of three or four, each player's strokes are calculated against the lowest handicap in the group, not against each other individually.

Common questions

Who gives strokes and who receives them?

The lower handicap player gives strokes to the higher handicap player. The number is the difference between their two course handicaps. A 5 playing a 15 gives 10 strokes. A 12 playing an 18 gives 6 strokes. Nobody "loses" anything — the higher handicap player simply gets credit on the harder holes.

What if we have the same handicap?

You play even — no strokes exchanged, gross scores are net scores. Essentially scratch play between the two of you regardless of your actual index. Very common in casual rounds where players have played together enough to calibrate their games.

Can I use an estimated handicap in a casual round?

Absolutely — most groups do this all the time. For formal competitions and club events, an official index is required. But for a weekend round with friends, a reasonable estimate based on typical scores is perfectly fine and makes the round more competitive and interesting for everyone.

What's the difference between a course handicap and a playing handicap?

They're often used interchangeably in casual play. Technically, a playing handicap is the course handicap adjusted for the specific format — some competitions apply a percentage (like 90% of course handicap). For most casual rounds, course handicap and playing handicap are the same thing.

Estimates are approximations and not an official handicap index.
