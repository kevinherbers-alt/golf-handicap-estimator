Which Golf Rounds Count Toward Your Handicap Index?

One of the most confusing parts of the handicap system — finally explained clearly.

If you've ever gotten an official handicap and wondered why some rounds seem to matter and others don't, you're not alone. The rules about which scores to post are one of the most misunderstood parts of the World Handicap System, and they catch experienced golfers off guard regularly.

The short answer: you're supposed to post every acceptable round you play. But "acceptable round" has a specific meaning.

What makes a round acceptable to post

Under the World Handicap System, a round is acceptable for handicap posting when it meets all of these conditions:

  • Played on a rated course. The course must have an official course rating and slope rating. Most established golf courses qualify; par-3 courses and executive courses often don't.
  • At least 7 holes completed for a 9-hole round, or at least 10 holes for an 18-hole round. If you're rained off after the 11th hole, you can still post what you played.
  • Played under the Rules of Golf. Casual mulligans, foot wedges, and preferred lies (unless officially in effect) technically make a round non-postable. In practice, most recreational golfers make judgment calls here.
  • Played with at least one other golfer. Lone practice rounds where you're just hitting shots don't count — someone needs to be able to attest to your score.
You should post both good rounds and bad rounds. The system is designed to handle high scores — each hole has a maximum score (Net Double Bogey) that limits how much any single bad hole can damage your index. Don't skip posting a blowup round.

Why your handicap uses your best rounds, not your average

This surprises a lot of golfers. Your handicap index is calculated from the best 8 of your most recent 20 score differentials — not your average across all 20. This is intentional: the WHS is measuring your demonstrated potential, not your typical performance.

The practical result: your handicap reflects what you're capable of on a good day. Most rounds you play won't match your eight best recent rounds. That's completely normal — routinely shooting better than your handicap suggests is called "playing to your handicap," and it's harder than it sounds. Most recreational golfers play over their handicap more often than not.

Scores that need adjustment before posting

Net Double Bogey (NDB) adjustment: Each hole has a maximum score equal to double bogey plus any handicap strokes you're entitled to on that hole. Scores higher than NDB get capped before the differential is calculated. This prevents one catastrophic hole from wrecking your index.

Playing Conditions Calculation (PCC): If the course was playing significantly harder or easier than normal on a given day — due to weather or setup — the system makes a statistical adjustment to all scores posted that day. This happens automatically; you don't need to do anything.

9-hole rounds

You can post 9-hole rounds separately. A standalone 9-hole score is calculated against the 9-hole rating and slope and counts as half a round in your record. The system can also combine two 9-hole rounds from the same day into a full differential. Playing 9 holes regularly? Those rounds absolutely count toward your index.

What doesn't count

Driving range sessions don't count. Rounds where you're hitting multiple balls or playing out of order don't count. Rounds on unrated courses don't count. A scramble or best-ball format doesn't count as an individual score (though some associations have specific rules about these).

Common questions

Do I have to post every round, even bad ones?

Yes — you're supposed to post all acceptable rounds, not just good ones. The Net Double Bogey cap protects you from truly disastrous holes, and bad rounds eventually cycle off your record as you play more. Selectively posting only good rounds is considered score manipulation and can trigger a Committee Review of your index.

What if I forget to post a round?

Post it as soon as you remember. Most handicap systems allow retroactive entry for recent rounds. The score will be dated as played. If significant time has passed, check with your association about their posting window.

Does a casual weekend round with friends count?

If you're on a rated course, keeping a legitimate score, and playing with at least one other person, yes. The round doesn't need to be a formal competition. Most casual recreational rounds qualify — they just need to be posted honestly.

What is a score differential?

A score differential is the number that compares your performance to the course difficulty. It's calculated as: (Adjusted Gross Score minus Course Rating) times 113, divided by the Slope Rating. The official WHS calculator runs this for you automatically — just enter your score and the course info.

Why does my handicap seem slow to respond to good rounds?

Because the system uses your best 8 of 20. If you already have 8 strong differentials in your record, one more good round might not change anything until it displaces an older good round. The system also applies a cap to prevent a single exceptional round from causing a dramatic index drop — it looks at whether the round is statistically unusual relative to your recent history.
